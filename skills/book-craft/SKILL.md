# Write and edit books

Nonfiction manuscript editing: developmental, argument-level, and line editing with the author's voice rules and approval gates built in. Use for book rewrites and any long-form manuscript work.

## Open the project law first

- Every book project keeps law at its root: `manifesto.md` (thesis,
  reader, promise, scope, open decisions), `voice.md` or style guide,
  `outline.md` (chapter cards with status flags and gaps), and
  `conventions.md` (decision log, appended never edited).

- In resume mode, read the law before touching anything, even for a
  narrow request. The law is what keeps chapter 9 consistent with a
  decision made in chapter 2 three sessions ago.

- For a new book, bootstrap the law first through an interview:
  thesis in one sentence, primary reader, the promise, scope
  boundaries, voice. No drafting until the thesis and voice gates
  are satisfied. There are no reasonable defaults for these; the
  author decides them.

## Diagnose before you draft

- Read the whole manuscript, or the whole chapter, before changing a sentence. First impressions of structure are only visible at full length.

- Write an editorial note before any rewrite: what is working, the two or three highest-leverage structural moves, and the questions only the author can answer. Keep it to a few paragraphs, not a checklist.

- Propose structural moves, discuss them, then act. Never reorder chapters, cut a section, or collapse an argument unilaterally. The author owns the architecture.

- Settle structure before sentences. Line-editing a chapter whose argument may move is wasted work, and polished prose makes bad structure harder to see.

## Work the rewrite ladder

- Pass 1, developmental. State the book's thesis in one sentence from what is on the page. If you cannot, the thesis is not on the page. Check that chapters build on each other toward it. If chapters can be reordered with no loss, the structure is not doing its job.

- Pass 1 continued. Give every chapter one job: a promise made to the reader and a payoff that keeps it. Flag chapters that promise one thing and teach another, teach without an example, or never land.

- Pass 2, argument and evidence. For each real claim ask: what exactly is asserted, what backs it, does the backing support the precise wording, what would a skeptical informed reader say, are limits disclosed. Big claims need more support than small ones.

- Pass 2 continued. Sweep for redundancy across chapters. Mark repeated passages so they can be cut or consolidated in one place, not trimmed in five.

- Pass 3, line edit. Cut throat-clearing openings. Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs. Break overloaded sentences. Vary sentence length. Fix unclear pronoun referents. Keep terminology consistent.

- Pass 3 continued. Kill AI tells: hedged triplets, empty abstractions, sycophantic transitions, throat-clearing ("in today's world"), and words the author would never say aloud. If a sentence could appear in any book, it belongs in no book.

- Pass 4, proof. Mechanics last. There is no point perfecting punctuation in a paragraph that may be cut.

## Protect the author's voice

- Books are written in the author's voice, not an editor's. Draft from the author's words: interview them section by section, record answers verbatim, and build from those. See the `client-voice-copy` skill for the full method.

- Never use em dashes in the author's copy unless the author's style guide says otherwise. Check every draft for strays before showing it.

- Language and clarity fixes are yours to make silently. Anything that adds, changes, or removes what the author is actually saying needs their approval first. When they push back, ask which of their words you misused instead of rewriting cold.

- Work one chapter at a time. Get approval on a chapter, then treat it as a fixed anchor while later chapters are drafted.

## Build chapters that hold

- Open in the right place, which is usually later than the draft does. Cut the warmup and start where the chapter's real work begins.

- Escalate, do not accumulate. Each chapter should answer a reader question and raise a tougher one, so the middle of the book has pressure instead of padding.

- End on the strongest note. Do not trail off and do not wrap up too neatly; hand the reader a reason to start the next chapter.

- Scale examples up as the book proceeds: simple wins early, complex cases with tradeoffs later. Put proof where the reader needs confidence, not chapters later.

## Run the quality gates

- Read the chapter aloud. Awkward rhythm, repeated words, and dead sentences are audible before they are visible.

- Run the one-sentence thesis test on the book and the one-job test on each chapter. Anything that fails either test gets restructured, not polished.

- Run the reorder test: if two chapters can swap with no loss, merge them or give each a distinct job.

- Track throughlines with a simple grid: thesis, supporting pillars, recurring examples. Pages with no color are drift. Cut them or give them a job.

- Keep a style sheet for the manuscript: terminology decisions, name spellings, and recurring phrases. Consistency is a proof-stage job, but the sheet starts on day one.

## Finish with a blind read

- Once the manuscript is fully assembled and every chapter pass is complete, run an acquiring-editor assessment as the terminal gate. It edits nothing; it produces one candid report with a verdict.

- The read must be blind: the assessor opens only the built reading copy, never the manifesto, outline, conventions, or style guide. Those declare intent; the assessment judges what the manuscript communicates cold, the way every future reader meets it.

- The report spine: verdict (take on / revise / decline, no hedging), executive summary, what works (specific), what does not work yet (structural, each with its editorial consequence), the real book inside the manuscript, required changes prioritized with blockers first, market and readership, one next action, and a direct letter to the author.
